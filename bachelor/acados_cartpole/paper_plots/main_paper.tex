\begin{abstract}
Building an accurate simulator is costly, but policies trained in a simplified
one often fail on the real system. Learning directly on hardware avoids this
sim-to-real gap. Model-free \ac{RL} can learn online, but it is
typically sample-inefficient and can be unstable.
\Ac{MPC} policies, in which a neural network sets the
parameters of an \ac{MPC} scheme given the current state, can improve sample
efficiency. So far, however, such policies have mainly been trained in
simulation. We learn them directly on hardware, where the
\ac{MPC} uses a simplified prediction model and learning targets closed-loop
performance rather than model accuracy. We train them from scratch on a
physical cart-pole with \ac{SAC}. The critic evaluates the
\ac{MPC} parameters instead of the control input. Actor and critic updates
therefore require no solver calls, which permits a high update-to-data ratio.
All \ac{MPC} policies learn reliably across runs, whereas model-free \ac{SAC}
stabilizes the pendulum in only two of five. At a control period of 50~ms,
learning makes the swing-up of the nominal \ac{MPC} faster and its
stabilization more reliable. With a prediction model that omits the pendulum, the learned
controller at 50~ms even matches the nominal full-model \ac{MPC} at 10~ms.
\ac{MPC} policies can
thus enable sample-efficient learning directly on hardware, even with a partial
prediction model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Learning controllers directly on hardware avoids both the cost of building an
accurate simulator and the sim-to-real gap of a simplified one. Recent
off-policy \ac{RL} methods have made this feasible for locomotion and
manipulation within minutes to an hour of
interaction~\cite{smith2022walk,bohlinger2025gait,luo2024serl}. Real-world
data, however, remains expensive, and model-free \ac{RL} often needs many
samples and learns unreliably~\cite{dulacarnold2019challenges}.

\Ac{MPC} computes control inputs from a prediction model instead of learning
them from interaction, but model errors and tuning limit its closed-loop
performance. Combining control and \ac{RL} has therefore become an active
research direction~\cite{reiter2025synthesis,vernade2026foundations}. One
approach lets a neural network set the parameters of an \ac{MPC} from the
current state, so that the \ac{MPC} itself becomes the
policy~\cite{gros2020datadriven,zarrouki2021weights,reiter2023hierarchical}. Such
\ac{MPC} policies can improve sample
efficiency~\cite{fichtner2025thesis,romero2025acmpc}, but they have mainly been
trained in simulation, in some cases with transfer to
hardware~\cite{romero2024acmpc,romero2025acmpc}. Learning them directly on hardware has received
little attention.

This paper studies this setting on a physical cart-pole. We train \ac{MPC}
policies from scratch on the hardware, using \ac{SAC} with a high
update-to-data ratio and asynchronous updates, and compare them with
model-free \ac{SAC} trained under identical conditions. To reflect settings in
which computation and modeling effort are limited, we deliberately use a coarse
control period and simplified prediction models, and test how much of the
resulting performance loss learning can recover. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We show that \ac{MPC} policies learn reliably on
    hardware across all runs, whereas model-free \ac{SAC} stabilizes the
    pendulum in only two of five. At a
    control period of 50~ms, learning makes the swing-up of the nominal
    \ac{MPC} faster and its stabilization more reliable.
  \item We show that a prediction model omitting the pendulum can still
    support successful learning and match the reward of a full-model nominal
    \ac{MPC} operating five times more frequently.
  \item We provide a practical training setup for learning \ac{MPC} policies
    on hardware, which decouples the policy rate from the control rate and
    runs learning and control asynchronously.
\end{enumerate}
We discuss related work in more detail in Sec.~\ref{sec:related}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Our approach uses an \ac{MPC} as the policy and lets \ac{RL} learn, from
data collected on the hardware, how to set its parameters. The \ac{MPC}
contributes structure from a simplified model, and off-policy \ac{RL} makes
efficient use of each sample. We first review \ac{SAC} and \ac{MPC}, then
define \ac{MPC} policies and describe how we train them on hardware.

\subsection{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning.}
\label{sec:bg-rl}

\ac{RL} learns a policy from interaction with a Markov decision process (MDP)
$(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,\rho_0,\gamma)$~\cite{sutton2018rl} with state
space $\mathcal{S}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, transition kernel
$P(\cdot\mid s,a)$, reward $r(s,a)$, initial state distribution $\rho_0$, and
discount factor $\gamma\in(0,1)$. We build on
\ac{SAC}~\cite{haarnoja2019sacapps}, a widely used off-policy actor-critic
method that also underlies the \ac{MPC} policy learning in
Sec.~\ref{sec:bg-saczop}. \ac{SAC} learns a stochastic policy
$\pi_\psi(a\mid s)$ with network parameters $\psi$ that maximizes the
entropy-augmented return
\begin{equation}
  J(\pi_\psi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\psi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t
  \big(r(S_t,A_t) + \alpha\,\mathcal{H}\big(\pi_\psi(\cdot\mid S_t)\big)\big)\Big],
  \label{eq:maxent}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\psi}$ denotes the expectation over trajectories
generated by $S_0\sim\rho_0$, $A_t\sim\pi_\psi(\cdot\mid S_t)$, and
$S_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid S_t,A_t)$. The temperature $\alpha>0$, adapted
automatically to a target entropy, weights exploration against reward. \ac{SAC} trains two critics $Q_{w_1}(s,a)$
and $Q_{w_2}(s,a)$ by soft Bellman regression on transitions from a replay
buffer and uses their minimum in the target and the actor update to reduce
overestimation. Being off-policy, \ac{SAC} can reuse each
transition for multiple gradient updates, which we heavily exploit in
Sec.~\ref{sec:training}.

\paragraph{Model predictive control.}
\label{sec:bg-mpc}

\ac{MPC} repeatedly solves a finite-horizon \ac{OCP} \cite{rawlings2017mpc}.
Given the current state $\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_x}$, it optimizes over
states $x_k\in\mathbb{R}^{n_x}$ and inputs $u_k\in\mathbb{R}^{n_u}$ in
\begin{mini}|s|[2]
  {x_{0:N},\,u_{0:N-1}}{V_\phi(x_N)+\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} l_\phi(x_k,u_k)}
  {\label{eq:ocp}}{}
  \addConstraint{x_0=\bar{x},\quad h_N(x_N)\le 0}{}{}
  \addConstraint{x_{k+1}=f(x_k,u_k),\quad h(x_k,u_k)\le 0,\quad}{}{k=0,\dots,N-1}
\end{mini}
with horizon $N$, stage and terminal costs $l_\phi$ and $V_\phi$
parameterized by $\phi\in[\underline{\phi},\overline{\phi}]$, a known, possibly simplified, discrete-time
model $f$, and path and terminal constraints $h$ and $h_N$. Only the first
optimal input $u_0^\star(\bar{x},\phi)$ is applied before the \ac{OCP} is
solved again at the next time step. In practice, the solver may return only an
approximate solution (App.~\ref{app:config}).

When an \ac{MPC} controls the system, the
MDP action is the applied control input, $a=u_0$, and the \ac{OCP} is
initialized with the MDP state, $\bar{x}=s$. If the prediction model describes
only part of the system, the \ac{OCP} receives only the corresponding
components of $s$, as for the cart-only model in Sec.~\ref{sec:models}.

\subsection{Learning MPC Policies from Real Experience}
\label{sec:bg-saczop}

Prior work mostly trains policies that contain an \ac{MPC} with simulated
experience generated by a model, i.e., in a simulator. We instead learn from
real experience gathered on the hardware, which \citet{sutton2018rl} call
direct \ac{RL}. The \ac{MPC} uses a simplified prediction model, which
limits its closed-loop performance. Rather than improving the model, we follow
the argument of~\cite{gros2020datadriven} and learn the \ac{MPC} parameters
to improve the closed-loop performance directly.

\paragraph{MPC policies.} An \ac{MPC} policy combines a neural network with
an \ac{MPC}: the network chooses the \ac{MPC} parameters from the current
state, and the \ac{OCP} computes the action. Formally, a stochastic parameter
policy $\varphi_\psi(\phi\mid s)$ samples a parameter at each decision step
$t$, and the \ac{OCP}~\eqref{eq:ocp} turns it into the applied action,
\begin{equation}
  \phi_t\sim\varphi_\psi(\cdot\mid S_t),\qquad
  A_t = u_0^\star(S_t,\phi_t).
  \label{eq:mpc-prior}
\end{equation}
The resulting distribution of actions defines the \ac{MPC} policy
$\pi_\psi(a\mid s)$, which acts in the same action space as a model-free
policy. In the taxonomy of~\cite{reiter2025synthesis}, it is a hierarchical
architecture with \ac{MPC} within the deployed policy. Since $\phi$ depends on
the current state, it can vary along the trajectory, unlike a static tuning,
e.g., by Bayesian optimization~\cite{marco2016automatic}.

\paragraph{Learning on the parameters.} With active constraints, $\pi_\psi$ may
have no Lebesgue density, and even otherwise its density is hard to
evaluate~\cite{gros2019towards,gros2021stochastic}. We therefore train $\varphi_\psi$ with \ac{SAC} instead. Treating the \ac{MPC}
as part of the environment~\cite{reiter2025synthesis} yields an MDP with
action $\phi$, transition kernel $P(\cdot\mid s,u_0^\star(s,\phi))$, and reward
$r(s,u_0^\star(s,\phi))$, in which the \ac{OCP} is solved inside each
environment step. Applying \ac{SAC} to this MDP learns the critics directly on the parameters:
each critic $Q_{w_i}$, $i=1,2$, minimizes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{L}_Q(w_i) &= \mathbb{E}\Big[\big(Q_{w_i}(S,\phi) - R
    - \gamma(1-D)\big(\min_{j=1,2}Q_{\bar w_j}(S',\phi')
    - \alpha\log\varphi_\psi(\phi'\mid S')\big)\big)^2\Big],\\
  &\qquad (S,\phi,R,S',D)\sim\mathcal{D},\quad \phi'\sim\varphi_\psi(\cdot\mid S'),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:param-losses}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ is the replay buffer, $R=r(S,u_0^\star(S,\phi))$ was
recorded during interaction, $D\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether $S'$ is
terminal, and $Q_{\bar w_j}$ are target networks. The
parameter policy is updated by minimizing
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{L}_\varphi(\psi) &= \mathbb{E}\big[\alpha\log\varphi_\psi(\phi\mid S)
    - \min_{i=1,2}Q_{w_i}(S,\phi)\big],\\
  &\qquad S\sim\mathcal{D},\quad \phi\sim\varphi_\psi(\cdot\mid S).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The critics thus evaluate the parameters directly. The
\ac{OCP} is solved once per environment step to act, but not in the actor and
critic updates, and unlike in differentiable
\ac{MPC}~\cite{amos2018diffmpc,romero2024acmpc}, no sensitivities of the
solution are required.

We build on the implementation in
\leapc~\cite{leapc}, an open-source library that combines \ac{MPC} and \ac{RL}
and solves the \acp{OCP} efficiently with \acados~\cite{verschueren2021acados},
and describe below how we adapt it to learning on
hardware. As common for \ac{SAC}~\cite{haarnoja2019sacapps}, the parameter
bounds $[\underline{\phi},\overline{\phi}]$ are enforced by a squashed
Gaussian.

\subsection{Sample-Efficient Real-World Training}
\label{sec:training}

Since hardware interaction is the scarce resource, we extract as much learning
as possible from each transition.

\paragraph{High update-to-data ratio.} Following~\cite{smith2022walk}, we
perform $G=20$ critic updates and one actor update per transition, i.e., an
update-to-data ratio of $G$. Updating the actor less often than the critics
lets the value estimates settle before the policy exploits them, which limits
overestimation bias. This ratio is affordable because the
updates require no solver calls (Sec.~\ref{sec:bg-saczop}): each costs as much
as for model-free \ac{SAC} with the same networks. A critic on the applied
action, $Q(s,a)$, would instead need the action $u_0^\star(S',\phi')$ in every
target, and an actor trained through it would need the sensitivities of
$u_0^\star$ with respect to $\phi$. With our batch size of 64, this amounts to
more than 1300 \ac{OCP} solves and 64 sensitivity computations per transition,
i.e., on the order of seconds at our solve times (Table~\ref{tab:eval_linear}),
compared with a control period of 50~ms.

To keep training stable at this ratio, we add layer
normalization after every hidden layer of the critics, which limits value
overestimation at high update-to-data ratios~\cite{ball2023rlpd}. In
preliminary runs without layer normalization, more critics improved learning
significantly. With layer normalization, however, two critics worked better:
fewer critics make each update block faster, and this outweighed the benefit of
a larger ensemble.

\paragraph{Asynchronous acting and learning.} A block of $G$ gradient updates can take longer than one control period. To
keep it from stalling the control loop, we run control and learning in two
concurrent threads. The acting thread stores each transition and continues
without waiting. The learner runs one update block per new transition as soon
as it arrives, and the acting thread always uses the latest policy weights.
Updating with every transition, rather than in large batches between
episodes, keeps learning stable~\cite{smith2022walk}. Unlike~\cite{westenbroek2022lyapunov},
which synchronizes the policy only at the start of each episode, our policy
thus already improves within an episode.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{System and Task}
\label{sec:hardware}
\label{sec:reward}

The plant is a low-cost, belt-driven cart-pole on a 1~m linear rail
(Fig.~\ref{fig:hardware}), driven by a 24~V brushed DC motor. The pole is a
0.36~m rod of 0.016~kg on a cart of 0.174~kg. All controllers output a cart
force, which we convert into a motor voltage by inverting an identified
actuation model that also compensates cart friction (App.~\ref{app:hardware}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\columnwidth]{images/my_setup_numbered.png}
  \caption{\note{caption from bachelor thesis. Perhaps better Photo with schematic}}
  \label{fig:hardware}
\end{figure}

Each episode starts with the pendulum hanging at rest at the rail center, and
the task is to swing it up and balance it upright. An episode ends after
10~s (200~RL steps) or when the cart reaches the travel limit of $\pm0.43$~m,
where a safety layer disables the motor. We refer to the latter as a
travel-limit hit. A reset routine then drives the cart back to the center and
waits until the pendulum hangs at rest. All methods share the reward
\begin{equation}
  r = \underbrace{h(\theta)\big[1-\kappa_{\mathrm{top}}(\theta)
      \big(1-\kappa_{\mathrm{calm}}(\dot\theta)\big)\big]}_{\text{swing-up}}
    + \underbrace{3\,\kappa_{\theta}(\theta)\,\kappa_{\dot\theta}(\dot\theta)}_{\text{balancing}},
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $h(\theta)=\tfrac12(1+\cos\theta)\in[0,1]$ is the normalized pole height
and $\kappa_i$ are Gaussian kernels (App.~\ref{app:reward}). The swing-up term
rewards the height of the pole, with a velocity penalty that acts only near the
top, and the balancing term is a wide bonus for calm balancing. Since the
reward is non-negative, a travel-limit hit implicitly costs all future reward.
The \ac{MPC} policies also learned with considerably simpler rewards, but
\ac{SAC} did not. We therefore shaped the reward until \ac{SAC} learned, first
in simulation and then on the hardware, so the comparison is, if anything,
biased in favor of \ac{SAC}. Apart from this reward design, nothing was
transferred from simulation.

\subsection{Controllers}
\label{sec:models}
\label{sec:interfaces}

The \ac{MPC} policies use one of two prediction models. The full model
describes the cart-pole with state $x=(p,\theta,\dot p,\dot\theta)$, where $p$
is the cart position and $\theta$ the pole angle with $\theta=0$ upright, and
input $u=F$, the force on the cart. Treating the pole as a point mass $m$ at
distance $l$ from the pivot on a cart of mass $M$ gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \ddot p &= \frac{F - m l\,\dot\theta^2\sin\theta
            + m g\sin\theta\cos\theta}{M + m\sin^2\theta},\quad
  \ddot\theta = \frac{F\cos\theta - m l\,\dot\theta^2\sin\theta\cos\theta
            + (M+m)\,g\sin\theta}{l\,(M + m\sin^2\theta)},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cartpole}
\end{equation}
which we discretize with one RK4 step per shooting interval. The model neglects
friction and the rotational inertia of the rod. The
\emph{cart-only} model drops the pendulum and keeps the cart as a double
integrator, $\ddot p = F/M$, with state $(p,\dot p)$. Its \ac{OCP} receives only
the cart states, while the policy still observes the full state. Both models
use the constraints $|p_k|\le0.37$~m, a 6~cm margin to the travel limit, and
$|F_k|\le20$~N, no terminal constraint, and the tracking cost
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  l_\phi(x,u) &= \tfrac{1}{2}\|x - x_{\mathrm{ref}}\|_{\Lambda}^2
                         + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_u u^2,\\
  V_\phi(x)   &= \tfrac{1}{2}\|x - x_{\mathrm{ref}}\|_{\Lambda}^2,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
with $x_{\mathrm{ref}}=(p_{\mathrm{ref}},\theta_{\mathrm{ref}},0,0)$,
$\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(10^3,10^3,1,1)$ for the full model,
$x_{\mathrm{ref}}=(p_{\mathrm{ref}},0)$, $\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(10^3,1)$ for the
cart-only model, and $\lambda_u=0.1$. The cart-only \ac{OCP} is thus a convex QP.

Each \ac{MPC} policy learns one entry of $x_{\mathrm{ref}}$ as the scalar $\phi$,
shared across all stages, with the other entries fixed at zero
(Table~\ref{tab:methods}). \zopangle learns $\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and \zoppos
learns $p_{\mathrm{ref}}$, both with the full model, and \zopcart learns
$p_{\mathrm{ref}}$ with the cart-only model. For \zopangle and \zoppos,
$\phi=0$ recovers the nominal \ac{MPC}, which already swings up the pendulum.
The cart-only \ac{OCP}, in contrast, knows nothing about the pendulum, so
\zopcart must induce both the swing-up and the stabilization through the cart
reference. \zopangleten runs the \ac{MPC} at 10~ms while the policy still
decides every 50~ms, holding $\phi$ for $K=5$ \ac{MPC} solves
(App.~\ref{app:macro}). As baselines, the nominal \acp{MPC} \mpcfifty and
\mpcten fix $\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}=p_{\mathrm{ref}}=0$, and model-free \ac{SAC}
outputs the force directly. With a fixed reference, the cart-only \ac{OCP}
never swings up the pendulum, so \zopcart has no nominal counterpart. All
\acp{OCP} are solved with \acados via \leapc with $N=8$ shooting intervals over a
horizon of 0.45~s, chosen only so that a solve fits into the control period
(App.~\ref{app:config}).

\subsection{Training and Evaluation}
\label{sec:protocol}
\label{sec:config}

Each learning controller is trained from scratch on the hardware for
$10^4$~RL steps with 5~seeds, and \ac{SAC} and the \ac{MPC} policies share all
hyperparameters (App.~\ref{app:config}). One RL step corresponds to 50~ms for
all learning controllers, so $10^4$ steps are 8.3~min of interaction, and all
learning curves share one time axis. During training, we record the
stabilization rate, i.e., the fraction of episodes in which the pole stays
within $\pm0.15$~rad of upright for 2~s. It uses the stochastic policy and is
thus conservative. After training, we evaluate the final policy of the
median-performing seed of each learning controller, as well as both nominal
\acp{MPC}, deterministically in 20~runs each. For the learned policies, this
means using the squashed mean of the policy instead of sampling.

We attribute the gap to SAC to two effects. First, SAC has to learn the track

\subsection{(Q2) Effect of the Learned Reference}
\label{sec:res-interfaces}

The variants mainly differ in how fast and how reliably learning succeeds
(Fig.~\ref{fig:learning}). \zoppos is the fastest to its first success, after
only 32~s on average, but never surpasses \mpcfifty. \zopangle needs 1.8~min to
its first success but stabilizes reliably from 2.5~min on. \zopcart is the
slowest to its first success (3.5~min), but over the final 50~s its
stabilization rate is 100\,\% for every seed. At 10~ms, \zopangleten combines
a fast start (1.5~min) with reliable stabilization.

These differences reflect how much of the task each prior already solves and
how much room it leaves. In \zoppos, the OCP always drives the pole upright,
so swing-up and stabilization are built into the prior and the first success
comes fastest. Why it does not surpass \mpcfifty despite a strongly
state-dependent reference remains open.
\zopangleten starts from the strongest prior but differs from the other
variants in both $K$ and the control period, so we cannot attribute its fast
start to either alone. \zopcart has the weakest prior and must learn the whole
task itself, consistent with its late first success. Its reliable
stabilization at the end may stem from its convex OCP, which converges at
every step (Table~\ref{tab:eval_linear}), and the policy's full authority over
the swing-up. The final evaluation is consistent with this picture: \zopcart reaches the
highest mean total reward, on par with \mpcten, while \zoppos stays at the
level of \mpcfifty. Since only the median seed of each variant is evaluated,
the ranking among the remaining variants is uncertain.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Our work lies at the intersection of \ac{RL} on real systems
and the combination of \ac{MPC} and \ac{RL}.

\textbf{Real-world RL.} Off-policy actor-critic methods with high
update-to-data ratios and regularized critics learn quadruped locomotion
directly on hardware within 20~min~\cite{smith2022walk} and, more recently,
under 8~min~\cite{bohlinger2025gait}, and comparable pipelines solve contact-rich
manipulation tasks within an hour~\cite{luo2024serl}. On a physical swing-up
pendulum, in contrast, plain deep RL failed on hardware although the same task
was solvable in simulation~\cite{bohm2022lowcost} \note{maybe this is littel bit cherry picked source, other manage to learn a good policy ofc.}. Where prior knowledge is
added, it enters through the reward or through pretraining:
Westenbroek et al.~\cite{westenbroek2022lyapunov} shape the reward with a
control Lyapunov function and fine-tune a simulation-pretrained cart-pole
swing-up with seconds of real data. In all of these works, state constraints
are handled only implicitly through rewards and terminations.

\textbf{MPC-RL.} Among the combinations surveyed in~\cite{reiter2025synthesis},
we follow the line in which a learned policy parameterizes an MPC that acts as
the actor. Gros and Zanon~\cite{gros2020datadriven} showed that adapting the
MPC cost and constraints by RL can recover the optimal policy despite model
mismatch. Such parameter adaptation has since been applied to weights-varying
MPC for autonomous vehicles~\cite{zarrouki2021weights,zarrouki2024safe},
strategic racing with a critic on the MPC parameters~\cite{reiter2023hierarchical},
and SAC with MPC policies~\cite{fichtner2025thesis}, all trained in
simulation. AC-MPC~\cite{romero2024acmpc,romero2025acmpc} embeds a
differentiable MPC in the actor and transfers zero-shot to a quadrotor racing
at up to 21~m/s, but training itself also happens in simulation. Learning on
the real system has been shown for a value function that serves as the MPC
cost of a ground vehicle~\cite{karnchanachari2020practical} and for a Gaussian
process model inside receding-horizon iLQR, which swings up a real cart-pole
from scratch within a few episodes~\cite{boedecker2014approximate}. Both
replace a component of the MPC by a learned function rather than learning a
policy over the parameters of a given formulation, which is what we learn
directly on hardware.

\section{Hardware Platform}
\label{app:hardware}
The pole is a 0.36~m rod of 0.016~kg on a cart of 0.174~kg, which also
parameterize the model~\eqref{eq:cartpole}. Cart position is measured by a
Calt~GHH60 incremental optical encoder and pendulum angle by an AMS~AS5600
magnetic encoder. Velocities are estimated by finite differences with
exponential-moving-average filtering, without further state estimation.

Control is split between two nodes. An Arduino Due reads the sensors, applies
the motor voltage commanded by the host, and runs a low-level safety layer
that disables the motor when the cart passes the travel limit of
$\pm0.43$~m. A host PC evaluates the policy, solves the MPC,  and converts the
commanded force into a voltage. Both nodes run non-blocking and independently
in time, similar to~\cite{bohm2022nonblocking}, so that variable delays never
stall the control loop.

Since all controllers output a cart force but the driver accepts a voltage
$U$, we identify the lumped actuation model
\begin{equation}
  \ddot p = k_U U - k_v\dot p - k_c\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot p),
  \label{eq:actuation}
\end{equation}
which captures the motor gain together with viscous and Coulomb friction of
the cart. Fitting~\eqref{eq:actuation} to constant-voltage step responses by
bound-constrained nonlinear least squares yields the gain $k_U=5.28$ and the
friction coefficients $k_v=48.45$ and $k_c=7.75$ in SI units. Online, we
invert it to $U=\big(F/M+k_v\dot p+k_c\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot p)\big)/k_U$ for the
commanded force $F$ and clip $U$ to the admissible voltage range. Friction is
thus compensated at the actuator but absent from the prediction model
(Sec.~\ref{sec:models}).

\section{MPC and Learning Configuration}
\label{app:config}
We formulate the OCPs with \leapc~\cite{leapc} and solve them with
\acados~\cite{verschueren2021acados} using multiple shooting with $N=8$
intervals over a horizon of 0.45~s, a Gauss--Newton SQP method, and the QP
solver HPIPM~\cite{frison2020hpipm}, warm-started from the previous solution.
We kept the MPC tuning deliberately minimal: the horizon and the iteration
limits were chosen only so that a solve fits into the control period, not to
maximize performance, and are the same at both control periods. Each solve is
capped at 10 SQP iterations of at most 20 QP iterations each. If the SQP has
not converged by then, the solver returns its current iterate, which need not
satisfy the constraints, and this input is applied. The cap keeps the
computation time per step at 0.6--2.1~ms (Table~\ref{tab:eval_linear}), which
leaves the host CPU to the learner running in parallel and keeps the setup
representative of more complex systems, where the per-step budget is tight.

{\color{magenta}\paragraph{Networks.} Actor and critics are multilayer perceptrons with
\todo{$n_\mathrm{l}$} hidden layers of \todo{$n_\mathrm{h}$} units and
\todo{ReLU} activations. The actor maps the state $s$ to the mean and log
standard deviation of a Gaussian, which is squashed by $\tanh$ and scaled to
the bounds of its output, i.e., the force $F$ for \ac{SAC} and the parameter
$\phi$ for the \ac{MPC} policies (Table~\ref{tab:methods}). Each of the two
critics maps $(s,a)$ for \ac{SAC}, or $(s,\phi)$ for the \ac{MPC} policies, to a
scalar, with layer normalization after every hidden layer
(Sec.~\ref{sec:training}). The actor uses no normalization. Each critic has a
target network updated by Polyak averaging.}

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.} SAC and the MPC policies share all learning
hyperparameters: discount factor 0.99, Adam with learning rates $10^{-4}$ for
actor and critics and $10^{-3}$ for the temperature, batch size 64, a replay
buffer of $10^6$ transitions, Polyak factor 0.005, and target entropy $-1$.
{\color{magenta}Per transition, the learner performs $G=20$ critic updates and one actor and
temperature update.} Learning starts after 10~s of interaction.

\section{Decoupled Policy and Control Rates}
\label{app:macro}
\label{sec:rates}
We fix the policy decision interval to 50~ms in all experiments and run the MPC at the same rate by default ($K = 1$). This keeps the computational demands within reach of MPC formulations for more complex systems, where real-time solves at high rates are often infeasible, and makes our findings more likely to transfer. When the MPC can be solved faster, as in our cart-pole setup, the control rate can be raised without increasing the policy rate: every $K$ control steps the actor selects a new parameter $\phi$, which is held constant while the MPC re-solves \eqref{eq:ocp} at
every step. Keeping the policy rate low is deliberate, since at high rates consecutive states differ only marginally and individual transitions carry little learning signal~\cite{sharma2017figar,metelli2020persistence}. Contrary to classical action repetition~\cite{mnih2015dqn,sharma2017figar}, only the parameter policy $\phi$ is delayed, so fast feedback and constraint enforcement are retained through the MPC controller running at every step.

For $K > 1$, each cycle yields one macro-transition
$(s_t, \phi_t, \bar{r}_t, s_{t+K})$, where $\bar{r}_t$ is the mean of the $K$ per-step rewards. The mean keeps the reward scale independent of $K$ and does not miss short reward events, as a single boundary sample could. If
an episode terminates mid-cycle, the mean is taken over the executed steps. Figure~\ref{fig:macro} illustrates one cycle for $K=5$, as used
by \zopangleten.
